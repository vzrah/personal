THE BLACK DEATH | Research Notes

Year 8 History • Medieval Europe • Assessment Task 2

Core argument to adapt

  • Demographic catastrophe reshaped work, society and religious life.
  • Labour scarcity strengthened workers’ bargaining power in some places; authorities pushed back. A catalyst for change, not the sole cause of feudalism’s decline.

At a glance

  • 1347–51: first wave across Europe; later outbreaks returned.
  • Cause: Yersinia pestis bacterium.
  • Scale: Britannica estimates c. 25 million European deaths; often summarised as about one-third or more. Estimates vary by region.
  • Impact: all ranks lost family, leaders and workers.
  • Disease: bubonic plague commonly caused fever and painful swollen lymph nodes (“buboes”); illness could progress rapidly.
  • Name: “Black Death” is the later name for the catastrophic pandemic, not proof that everyone’s skin turned black.

Spread: routes and disease

  • Trade routes linked Asia, Black Sea ports and the Mediterranean; ships and travel carried plague between regions.
  • Reached Sicily in 1347; spread to Italy, France and Spain in 1348, then central and northern Europe.
  • Infected fleas associated with rodents carried Y. pestis.
  • Busy ports and towns connected by trade could be hit rapidly; local timing and severity differed.
  • Repeated outbreaks followed the first wave, including major recurrences in 1361–63 and later decades.
  • People then did not know the bacterial cause or modern germ theory.

Useful chronology

  • 1347: plague reaches Mediterranean ports; Sicily is among the first affected in Europe.
  • 1348–49: spreads through western and northern Europe, including England.
  • 1348: Jewish community at Tàrrega, Catalonia, suffers a documented violent assault amid plague-related persecution.
  • 1349 / 1351: England issues the Ordinance, then Statute of Labourers.
  • 1361 onward: recurring waves cause further deaths; the crisis is not limited to 1347–51.

Economic effects

  • Deaths caused worker shortages in farming, crafts and services.
  • Some survivors negotiated better pay; landowners competed for workers.
  • England’s 1351 Statute sought to cap wages and compel work.
  • Meaning: authorities feared workers’ leverage; the law’s existence does not prove it worked.
  • With fewer people to feed, some places saw lower demand for grain and falling rents; local outcomes varied.
  • Survivors could inherit land, tools or property when families died without heirs.
  • Labour relations shifted unevenly; serfdom did not end immediately. Later unrest had several causes, not plague alone.

Social effects

  • Mortality disrupted households and communities; some farms and businesses lacked workers.
  • Survivors able to move or negotiate could gain opportunities.
  • Some villages were abandoned or shrank; urban areas and countryside were both affected, with different local death rates.
  • False accusations fuelled antisemitic attacks on Jewish communities; Jews did not cause the plague.
  • Effects varied by region, wealth and occupation.
  • In Tàrrega, six communal graves contained victims of violent deaths; archaeology supports written accounts of the 1348 assault.

Religious effects

  • Prayer, processions and penance sought spiritual protection.
  • Flagellants whipped themselves as penance; not all Christians did this.
  • Clergy died and the Church could not stop plague; faith nevertheless remained central.
  • Shortage of priests could disrupt services and pastoral care; some survivors questioned religious explanations or leaders.
  • Responses were mixed: people might pray, seek religious help, flee, or follow local customs.

Long-term significance

  • Repeated outbreaks prolonged the demographic shock and slowed population recovery.
  • Population loss changed the balance between available land and scarce labour.
  • Higher worker bargaining power challenged old obligations; rulers and landowners tried to reassert control.
  • A catalyst for change, not an instant end to feudalism; effects varied across Europe.

Evidence & Response Plan

Dot-point reminders • Use your own words • Add the question’s specific focus

Three body-point options

  • 1 — Economic: deaths → labour shortage → bargaining power → wage controls.
  • 2 — Social: mass death → disruption and new openings; fear → scapegoating.
  • 3 — Religious: spiritual responses and penance; varied reactions and continuing faith.

TEEL paragraph checklist

  • T — Topic: impact supporting your argument.
  • E — Explain: cause, change and people affected.
  • E — Evidence: example, date, source or statistic.
  • L — Link: significance; connect to question.

Evidence you can explain

  • Population: Britannica estimates c. 25 million European deaths; not an exact count.
  • Law: 1351 Statute says able-bodied workers could be required to serve and wages should not exceed customary rates.
  • Inference: the law describes labour scarcity and workers seeking higher wages; authorities feared shortages of ploughmen and servants.
  • Limit: one English law is evidence about English policy, not proof of conditions across Europe or successful enforcement.
  • Persecution: archaeology and texts document the 1348 assault on Tàrrega’s Jewish community.
  • Spread: port-to-port and inland spread helps explain the link between trade, movement and rapid transmission.

Source use: quick evaluation

  • Statute: primary source for lawmakers’ aims, not proof of successful enforcement.
  • Authors: king and parliament wrote law to address a perceived labour problem; it reflects elite priorities.
  • Modern histories: combine written evidence and research; estimates remain uncertain.
  • Archaeology: graves provide physical evidence; compare them with written accounts for context.
  • Perspective: compare region, date, author and purpose before generalising across Europe.

Planning the response

  • Question: underline command word (e.g. explain / assess) and focus (economic / social / overall).
  • 5-minute plan: position + three reasons + one example each.
  • Introduction: brief context + direct answer + preview of points.
  • Body: three TEEL paragraphs; explain cause, change and consequence.
  • Conclusion: sum up; no new evidence.
  • 45 minutes: save time to check structure, terms, names and dates.
  • Use evidence: introduce the example, explain what it shows, then link it to your argument.
  • Be precise: say “in England” for the Statute; use “some” and “varied” where effects differed.

Guiding questions to research

  • What was the Black Death? How did it spread, and how many people were affected?
  • How did labour scarcity affect workers, peasants, landowners and everyday life?
  • How did people respond religiously? What changed over time, and why was it significant?

Key terms

  • Pandemic: epidemic across regions or continents.
  • Labour shortage: too few available workers.
  • Bargaining power: ability to negotiate pay or conditions.
  • Serfdom: peasants tied to a lord’s land and obligations.
  • Scapegoating: falsely blaming a group for crisis.
  • Mortality: deaths in a population during a period.
  • Primary source: evidence created during the period studied.

Avoid these overclaims

  • “Exactly one-third everywhere” — estimates and death rates varied.
  • “Plague ended feudalism” / “Church collapsed” — change was gradual; faith continued.
  • “Jews spread plague” — false antisemitic accusation.
  • “Everyone gained” / “the law worked” — opportunity varied; law shows intent, not outcome.

Research sources

  • Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Black Death” — timeline, European death estimate, trade routes and plague vectors.
  • Yale Avalon Project, “The Statute of Labourers; 1351” — English primary-source text.
  • Colet et al., “The Black Death and Its Consequences for the Jewish Community in Tàrrega” (2014) — written and archaeological evidence.
